EverWeave / The Summit opportunity
Exhibit 2 & Part II

The Summit opportunity

A national retailer proposes a three-year partnership. The founders must decide what EverWeave can support and what it should protect.

On this page

The proposalProposed termsWhat remains undecidedThe founders’ discussion

Proposed Retail Partnership

Summit Trail Outfitters, a national outdoor retailer, has approached EverWeave about establishing a three-year retail partnership.

Summit has researched EverWeave’s products, customer base, business philosophy, and commitment to U.S.-made products. The retailer believes EverWeave would complement its existing outdoor apparel offerings and appeal to customers seeking quality, durable products from a growing domestic brand.

Why Summit Is Interested

Summit views EverWeave as a company with an established identity, loyal customers, quality products, and a record of steady growth.

The proposed partnership is intended to expand EverWeave’s national reach while allowing Summit to offer customers a U.S.-made outdoor brand with an established reputation.

Summit is proposing a curated assortment, not EverWeave’s entire product catalog. This approach would allow both companies to focus on products they believe are best suited for a national retail rollout.

What the Proposal Does Not Determine

The proposal establishes the basic framework for a potential partnership, but it does not determine how EverWeave should prepare internally for the additional business.

Decisions involving staffing, training, production planning, inventory, technology, supplier coordination, marketing resources, and other operational needs would remain EverWeave’s responsibility.

Part II · The Opportunity

The Summit Trail Outfitters proposal gave Emily, Marcus, and Sarah plenty to think about.

They had considered what national growth might eventually look like for EverWeave, but Summit made that possibility much more immediate. Three hundred stores, a three-year commitment, and approximately $1.8 million in annual wholesale purchases represented a significant opportunity for a company that had grown largely through direct-to-consumer sales and relationships with independent retailers.

Emily was the first to break the silence.

“This could put EverWeave in front of people who have never heard of us.”

She could already imagine customers discovering the brand in Summit stores across the country and then returning to EverWeave directly for future purchases. The coordinated marketing and online exposure were appealing as well. To Emily, Summit offered more than additional sales. It could accelerate national awareness of the EverWeave brand.

“But I don’t want us to get so excited about 300 stores that we underestimate what it takes to supply 300 stores,” Marcus responded.

He looked again at the estimates he had prepared.

“The $1.8 million sounds great—and it is significant. But that’s revenue. It isn’t $1.8 million that drops to the bottom line.”

Emily nodded. She knew where Marcus was going.

“We would have to invest before we see much of that revenue,” she said.

“Exactly,” Marcus replied. “Inventory. Production capacity. People. Technology. Training. Working capital. We can do the work. My question is whether we can do it well without putting unnecessary pressure on the rest of the business.”

Sarah had been thinking about a different kind of capacity.

“And on the people,” she added. “More business doesn’t just mean more products moving out the door. Someone has to manage the orders. Someone has to work with Summit. Our manufacturing partners have to respond. Employees have to learn new processes. Customer service may change. Even a good opportunity can create stress if we aren’t prepared for it.”

Emily leaned back.

“So we could be looking at a very good opportunity that we’re simply not ready for?”

“Maybe,” Marcus said. “Or a good opportunity that we need to prepare for differently.”

Sarah nodded. “Those aren’t the same thing.”

That distinction mattered.

The founders were not debating whether growth was good or bad. EverWeave had been growing for years, and each of them wanted the company to continue moving forward. The question was what kind of growth would strengthen the company rather than stretch it beyond what it could support.

Emily returned to the proposal.

“I also don’t want us to overlook what Summit has done here. They didn’t come to us asking us to change who we are. They’ve researched us. They’re willing to work with a curated product line. They’re keeping the U.S.-made commitment. And they’re talking about three years, not one season.”

“That matters,” Sarah said. “So do our independent retailers.”

The room grew quieter.

EverWeave had built important relationships with independently owned outdoor stores. Many of those retailers had supported the brand while it was still relatively unknown. A national partnership could introduce EverWeave to thousands of new customers, but the founders needed to consider how those existing partners might view the change.

“Would Summit help those relationships because more people know our name?” Emily asked. “Or would some of our retailers see Summit as competition?”

“Possibly both,” Sarah said.

Marcus smiled. “That’s helpful.”

“It wasn’t supposed to be,” Sarah replied.

They laughed.

It was a familiar pattern. Emily pushed them toward possibility. Marcus asked what the numbers and operations could support. Sarah made sure they considered the people and relationships affected by the decision. Their different perspectives did not eliminate disagreement, but they usually made their decisions better.

Marcus looked back at his calculations.

“I keep coming back to the same thing. I don’t think this is simply a yes-or-no decision.”

Emily looked at him. “Meaning?”

“If we say no because we’re uncomfortable with the size of the opportunity, we may walk away from something that could be very good for EverWeave. But saying yes just because the revenue and national exposure look attractive doesn’t make sense either.”

Sarah nodded. “There may be things we would need to change, negotiate, phase in, or protect.”

“Right,” Marcus said. “So maybe that’s the question we need to answer before anything else.”

He looked across the table at Emily and Sarah.

“What would have to be true for us to say yes?”

The room grew quiet.
